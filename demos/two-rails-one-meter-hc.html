Two Rails, One Meter

Public money moves on one rail; the public record moves on another. They share a single blockchain relay and a single meter — and the meter counts only the record. Not one cent of the money that crosses this diagram is billable.

The whole system, one picture

Two products share one spine. Above the spine, dollars move through Modern Treasury and two ledgers, ending in a vendor's bank account. Below it, records move — bank files, notices, filings — and no money moves at all. Both hand work to the same anchor relay, and the meter sits at the far end of that relay, counting confirmed writes.

Rail 1, step one — getting a municipality's money in

Modern Treasury is our payment processor, not our bank-in-a-box, and that has one sharp consequence: there are no virtual accounts. A push payment lands on a shared organization account carrying no tenant key at all. So an inbound dollar has to be told who it belongs to, and there are only two honest ways to do that.

As currently wired, the automatic path cannot fire. The webhook handler builds its incoming-payment record from seven fields and never fills in the originating routing number or the masked account, and the matcher short-circuits to “no match” the moment either is empty. The matcher itself is sound; the event that feeds it is thin. Until that is closed, every push lands in the civic-admin queue — which fails safe, and looks exactly like a healthy system with nothing to match.
Two more things this rail does not do. No expected payment objects are ever created — reconciliation is driven entirely off incoming payment details. And Modern Treasury does not automatically ledger a payment order just because an internal account is linked to a ledger account; the only thing that ledgers a payment order is a ledger transaction attached at creation, behind its own opt-in flag. With that flag off, the MT-side cash mirror simply does not exist.

Rail 1, step two — the solicitation budget

Funding and budgeting are two different moves. Fund tops up a reusable escrow that belongs to the town and outlives any one procurement. Allocate earmarks part of it to a specific solicitation, and that earmark is the project's spending budget. Both are pure ledger movements — no coin, no chain, no wallet.

What allocating actually does

  • One balance-locked Modern Treasury ledger transaction: debit the town's escrow, credit the solicitation's earmark.
  • No bridge journal posting at all. No coin moved and no dollar of backing changed hands, so the accounting book has nothing to say.
  • The over-allocation gate belongs to MT, not to us — the debit carries an available-balance condition and MT rejects it atomically. Two concurrent awards cannot both win.
  • The escrow is reusable and per-town; the earmark is per-solicitation. One funding can back many procurements.

What it refuses, and why

  • A destination wallet address — funding credits a ledger balance and has no on-chain destination. Supplying one is an error, not an ignored field.
  • Self-custody mode, or any named chain — both rejected before a cent moves. The legacy path that converted and pushed coin on-chain is gone, not deprecated.
  • A solicitation whose ledger was never provisioned — ownership is then unprovable, so it fails closed rather than trusting the caller.
  • Another town's solicitation — the earmark account carries its owner, and a mismatch is refused and recorded.
A failed allocation poisons its own idempotency key. Replaying that key does not retry — it re-surfaces the failure. This is deliberate: the common cause is an escrow that was short, and silently retrying a money movement against a balance that has since changed is how you fund something twice. Retrying takes a fresh key, which is a decision somebody has to make.
One label on this path is a fiction, on purpose. Funding records its chain as polygon even though nothing settles on Polygon — the saga table's constraint only admits real network slugs, and the escrow path never reaches a chain at all. It is a schema artifact, and the code says so where it writes it.

The two books, and which one is the truth

One is Modern Treasury Ledgers; the other is the bridge's own double-entry journal. The instinct is that MT holds the cash and we hold the balances. It is the other way round: a counterparty's spendable balance is an MT ledger account, read live from MT, and the journal line that looks like that balance is a mirror of it.

There are no per-tenant virtual accounts, and there never will be. Modern Treasury is our payment processor and refuses virtual accounts on payment-account internal accounts. Every owner shares the organization's internal accounts; separation exists only in the ledger. Three drifts are sanctioned, not bugs. Card settlement debits the earmark with no balance lock, so a solicitation's budget can go negative and the nightly check only reports it. A payment order whose mirror fails to build is created unledgered rather than failed. And the card-authorization gate never calls MT at all — it reads a local mirror in the hot path, with the nightly reconciliation as the backstop. Each buys availability at the cost of a temporarily wrong number.

Rail 1, at rest — yield on money that is waiting

Between funding and spending, a town's money sits still — and it is already earning. The just-in-time conversion design exists precisely so the float stays in the interest-bearing account until the moment of spend. Today that return accrues to the platform. This adds a variable-rate pass-through, so it accrues to the municipality whose money it is.

Status: proposed. Everything above this line is in the tree today. The rate schedule, the accrual job and the two new accounts below are a design, not shipped code — drawn here so the seams it has to fit are visible.

The guards this has to inherit

Each of these is a defect this codebase has already paid for somewhere else. A new money path that does not adopt them re-earns the lesson.

Fail closed, do not accrue zero

A period with no rate row must refuse and raise, never accrue nothing. This is exactly the silent-zero class the billing meter already has a metric for: a statement showing no interest and a statement showing no configuration are byte-identical, and only one of them is a decision.

Idempotent, and therefore unforgiving

One accrual per owner per period, on conflict do nothing — the first write wins forever. That is the right shape, and it means a rate corrected after the fact has no backfill path. Which is precisely why the guard above is a refusal rather than a warning.

Deterministic key, not MT’s window

Modern Treasury’s own idempotency key expires after 24 hours. An accrual re-run a month later would post twice. The posting has to carry a deterministic key in our own forever-dedupe table, the way every other MT ledger movement here already does.

Floor the basis, and take no balance lock

Card settlement can drive an earmark negative by design. A negative basis would quietly charge a town interest, so the basis floors at zero. And the accrual is a credit — the available-balance constraint that guards allocation does not apply here and must not be copied across.

The constraint that decides whether this is sellable is not technical. Interest on public deposits is regulated, and the rules differ by jurisdiction — who may earn it, and where it must be remitted. Separately, if a town’s escrow ever holds bond proceeds, federal arbitrage-rebate rules can require excess earnings be handed back. That is why the rate schedule is keyed per owner rather than set globally: some towns will need a zero rate, and a zero that was chosen has to be distinguishable from a zero that was never configured. Confirm the treatment with counsel per state before enabling it for a tenant.

Rail 1, spending — the card

A solicitation budget drains two ways. One is the vendor payout in the next section. The other is a purchase card — and the card is the only instrument on this page with two parties on it: the municipality issues, funds, limits, freezes and closes it; the vendor carries it and spends. Every card hangs off a contract, and the budget it draws down belongs to the town.

Three domains, and only one of them can act

Municipality — the issuer

  • Issues the card against a contract. The caller never names the cardholder’s business; it is derived from the contract, so a card cannot be issued to the wrong company by asking.
  • Sets the category list on the solicitation. This is the only municipal route that changes merchant-category rules — the platform’s own category profiles are read-only to a town.
  • Bounds the spend twice. A per-card limit and window, under an outer ceiling: the sum of every open card’s limit on a contract may not exceed the card pool the town allocated at award.
  • Keeps the roster. The per-employee card list is a municipal roster of vendor employees, copied from the vendor’s own proposal team at award. The town adds the person and mints the card.
  • Freeze, close, refill. All lifecycle verbs are municipal, and the finance-only capabilities are separated from the ones every municipal seat gets — viewing cards and categories is base; managing them is not.

Vendor — the cardholder

  • Read-only on the card itself. The vendor’s screen renders its contracts, its cards and the category locks, and offers no lifecycle verb at all.
  • One write, and it is a request. The vendor can ask for a refill. The municipality decides, and approving is what moves money onto the card. That asymmetry is the whole relationship in one route.
  • Supplies identity evidence. Enrollment examines named officers and beneficial owners. Of its eight outcomes, three mean the vendor must act — and the mainline first outcome is one of them.

Platform — the cross-tenant view

  • A cross-tenant card console and an enrollment queue that spans towns.
  • The queue is admin-only and deliberately not readable by support, and it is the one place a rejected enrollment surfaces.
  • The bulk mint at award time is a platform-level call, capped at a hundred cards, with the awarded vendor as account holder.
Identity enrollment is not a gate, and assuming it is would be the natural mistake. A vendor with no enrollment still gets a card — account-holder resolution falls through to a legacy exempt holder. Enrollment upgrades the card’s business identity; it does not authorize issuance. The fall-through is deliberate: making it a hard requirement would stop card issuance on every deployment that has not enrolled anybody yet.
Two asymmetries inside enrollment that read backwards until you see why. A municipal seat may hold the submit capability and still never submit — one key covers both submit and refresh, and the handler refuses the submit arm for every municipal seat regardless of what they hold. And a municipal seat may initiate a document upload and receive the status and reason codes, but the upload credential itself is stripped: the write channel into a vendor’s identity file only ever reaches the vendor.
The category code leads a double life, and that is the interesting part. Before the purchase it is a control — the gate that can refuse. After settlement it becomes a disclosure: the code rides into the on-chain attestation line item, where it is what makes an amount mean anything. “$88.00” and “$88.00 at a restaurant” are different facts on a public ledger. The merchant handle travels with it, and stays stable across transactions where a printed descriptor does not — descriptors vary by terminal.

Two things in that table are not what the documentation says. A partial capture takes the same arm as a full one and posts a single held-to-settled pair — it does not release the uncaptured remainder back to available, which the accounting doc claims it does. And an incremental authorization is documented as a posting set but has no arm at all: it produces zero journal lines and never touches the hold record, so the held amount stays at the original figure. Its regression test asserts the transaction row and never a journal line, which is why it has stayed invisible.
Card spend is the one activity on this page with its own revenue model — interchange, a program fee and a per-capture network charge already monetize it, which is exactly why the settlement attestation it produces is excluded from the meter. Metering the anchor as well would charge twice for one purchase. It is the clearest case on the page of an exclusion that is a pricing decision rather than an oversight.
A card could mint successfully and still be dead — now closed. A card whose bridge registration failed is declined at the terminal as unknown, and it never reaches the first gate above. The mint is deliberately allowed to survive that failure, and it still is. What was wrong is that the outcome lived only in the mint response and a log line, so nothing on the card row recorded it and every screen drew a healthy card — the first symptom was a refused purchase, and the card appeared in no repair worklist.

The outcome is now persisted on the card and carried on every read path, in three states rather than two. The third one is the interesting one: every card issued before the column existed is unknown, because the fact was never recorded. A boolean would have forced a lie — false puts a warning on every healthy card in the estate, which is how a real warning becomes invisible, and true absolves exactly the dead cards the column exists to find. The vendor now sees a plain-language notice in the one place they would look, the operator gets an indexed worklist, and both resolve through the repair button that already existed.
One number here still has no tie-out. The per-project pool the reservation gate declines against is a fourth balance store with no sub-ledger integrity check behind it — and it is the number a cardholder feels at the till.

Rail 1, last step — paying the vendor

An approved work-completion record splits into two things that travel separately and must not be confused: the value, which never leaves the banking rails, and the record, which goes on chain carrying no value at all.

Rail 2 — TLaaS, where nothing moves but the record

A customer connects a bank account and points feeds at it. Five ingestion sources — a webhook, an uploaded file, a bank API pull, an outbound SFTP pull, and a hosted SFTP drop banks log into — all normalize into one transaction shape. The same transaction reported twice is anchored once. What reaches the chain is a hash and four safe fields; everything that identifies a person stays sealed in Postgres.

What reaches the chain

  • The SHA-256 commitment
  • warrant_id · account_code
  • amount_cents · previous_hash

Frozen by a golden test — it is the contract an outside verifier reproduces.

What never leaves Postgres

  • Payee legal names
  • Bank account numbers
  • Invoice text and free-text memos

Sealed in the ingest payload. The split happens at parse time, before anything is hashed.

Why the relay wallet has to exist. Modern Treasury is our payment processor, and it has no on-chain memo primitive: a stablecoin payment order names a receiving account, never a raw address, and MT will only send from an internal account. So an owner's MT-registered address can receive money and can never sign an attestation. A platform wallet signs instead — and is deliberately not registered with MT, so relay identity and money identity stay separate.

Where metering occurs

There is one billable unit in the whole platform: a validated on-chain write of a billable record type. Not a dollar moved, not a file ingested, not a form filed — a confirmed anchor. Everything else on this page is unpriced.

Five of the seven belong to TLaaS; only two come off the procurement rail, and both are records of a decision rather than of a payment. civic_form is priced at an explicit zero — a deliberate decision written down, because a missing rate cell also bills nothing and the two would otherwise be indistinguishable.

What the money rail is never billed for

  • Funding the escrow — the whole on-ramp emits no chain write, so no meter is reachable.
  • Earmarking a solicitation budget — one Modern Treasury ledger movement, nothing on chain.
  • Yield accrued to a town — a ledger posting, so no billable unit exists for it. The platform earns the spread instead.
  • project_payout — the payout attestation is excluded by decision, not omission.
  • project_attestation — card spend is already monetized by interchange; metering the batch anchor would charge twice for one activity.
  • settlement — excluded, and separately produced by nothing.

The fee rails that do run on money

  • Card interchange — 180 bps, plus a 50 bps program fee and 10¢ per capture. This is where card spend earns.
  • MT_CIVIC_FEE_BPS — the only escrow-domain fee, charged at spend time in the daily card sync, never at fund or allocate. Defaults to 0.
  • ACH / wire origination — every knob defaults to 0, so a crypto payout attestation earns nothing at all.
  • Tier subscription — base_fee, plus an included-write allowance applied most-expensive-first.
Two switches make the whole meter silent. BILLING_ENABLED defaults to false in code, and users.pricing_tier_key has no default — a bridge user meters nothing until an operator assigns a tier. A municipality's billing subject is COALESCE(billing_user_id, bridge_user_id); when both are null, that town's on-chain writes are charged to nobody, silently.
